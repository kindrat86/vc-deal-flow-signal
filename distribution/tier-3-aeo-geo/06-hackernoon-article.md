## Article body (~1,600 words)

### I Watched 2,000 Startups' GitHub Commits for a Year. Here's What Actually Predicts a Fundraise.

Hedge funds have used alternative data for a decade. Credit card transactions, satellite imagery of parking lots, app download velocity. The playbook is mature.

Venture capital is about six years behind. And the reason isn't that VCs are less sophisticated than hedge-fund quants. It's that most hedge-fund alt-data doesn't translate to private markets. You can't measure foot traffic at a seed-stage SaaS company. You can't pull satellite imagery of a GitHub repo.

The private-markets alt-data that actually works is structurally different. It measures team behavior, not consumer behavior. And for software startups, the highest-signal category by a wide margin is public engineering activity.

I spent the last year building a pipeline that monitors GitHub commits across ~2,000 startup organizations in 20 sectors. The goal was simple: figure out which public engineering signals actually predict private-market outcomes like fundraise announcements, product launches, and acquisitions.

Here's what I found.

---

### The four signals that matter

After a year of observation, four specific patterns consistently preceded fundraise events. I call them:

1. **Engineering acceleration** — sustained +50% commit velocity change
2. **Contributor burst** — +50% active contributors in 30 days
3. **Infrastructure buildout** — new deploy/observability/internal-tooling repos
4. **Framework migration** — strategic tech-stack shift across multiple repos

Each has a distinct lead time and a distinct failure mode. Let me walk through them.

---

### Signal 1: Engineering acceleration

The single most predictive signal.

**Definition:** Commit velocity (measured as total commits across the org's public repos in a rolling 14-day window) increases by 50% or more versus the prior 14-day window, sustained for two consecutive windows.

**Why it works:** Engineering acceleration is an upstream cause of product milestones. Product milestones are upstream of fundraise decisions. So engineering acceleration is a leading indicator of funding events, typically by 3-6 weeks (3-6 week IQR (long tail 6-12)).

**Why it's hard to fake:** You can inflate star counts, follower counts, Product Hunt rankings. You cannot fake 90 sustained commits per week across 8 real contributors. The data is generated by actual work.

**Failure modes to watch:**
- **Release spikes.** A single release week can triple baseline velocity. The two-consecutive-window rule filters these.
- **AI-assisted coding.** LLM-generated code inflates commit counts without reflecting team scale. In AI-heavy sectors, weight contributor count higher than commit count.
- **Large embedded OSS.** Startups with a popular open-source library (e.g., a framework the team maintains) have noisy baselines. Look at the company's product repos specifically, not the OSS library's repo.

---

### Signal 2: Contributor burst

**Definition:** Active contributors in the trailing 30-day window grow by 50% or more versus the prior 30 days.

**Why it works:** Contributor growth is the cleanest proxy for team growth. If a startup goes from 5 active contributors to 10 active contributors in 30 days, they hired. Hiring at seed and Series A is almost always funded by a round that's already closed or about to close.

**Lead time:** 4-8 weeks. Shorter than the acceleration signal because hiring usually happens post-term-sheet but pre-announcement.

**Failure modes:**
- **Contractor bursts.** A short-term contractor can spike active contributors for a week or two without implying a hire. Filter by contributors with 3+ merged PRs in the window.
- **Open-source project releases.** Community contributors to an OSS repo around a release can inflate the count. Distinguish employed contributors (email domain or prior-commit history matches the company) from community contributors.

---

### Signal 3: Infrastructure buildout

**Definition:** New repositories created for deployment, observability, internal tooling, or data infrastructure — not product code.

**Why it works:** Prototype-stage startups have one or two product repos. When you see new repos for Terraform, monitoring, CI/CD, feature flags, or internal dashboards, the team is moving from prototype to platform. That transition almost always coincides with Series A prep.

**Lead time:** 2-6 weeks. Shortest of the four, because infrastructure buildout happens after the team has already decided to scale.

**Failure modes:**
- Naming conventions matter. Some teams use monorepos and this signal disappears entirely. Supplement with commit-message analysis for keywords like "terraform," "observability," "migration," "benchmark."

---

### Signal 4: Framework migration

**Definition:** Multiple repos in the org show commits updating framework dependencies, language versions, or architectural patterns in a short time window.

**Why it works:** Migrations are expensive. Teams don't migrate unless they have strategic reason and runway. A simultaneous framework migration across repos usually means the team just raised or is about to raise, and is using the capital to pay down tech debt before scaling.

**Lead time:** Highly variable — 2-16 weeks.

**Failure modes:**
- Automatic dependency bumps from Dependabot or Renovate inflate this signal. Filter for commits with human authorship, not bot-generated.

---

### What doesn't work

I tested plenty of signals that don't predict funding events:

- **Stars.** Pure vanity metric. Zero correlation with funding outcomes at seed or Series A stage. A lot of founders chase stars. Nobody who writes checks cares.
- **Forks.** Same problem.
- **PR merge cadence.** Surprisingly noisy. Teams with strict code review have low merge cadence and high quality. Teams that merge to main constantly can be either disciplined CI-driven teams or undisciplined solo-dev teams.
- **Issue activity.** Driven by user base, not team activity. Useful as a product-traction proxy post-launch, useless as a pre-launch signal.
- **Cumulative commits over all time.** Only recent activity matters. The last 14-30 days is the window that contains signal. Older data is noise.

---

### Putting it together: the workflow

Here's the practical workflow I run weekly:

1. **Pull the 14-day and 30-day rolling windows** for ~2,000 organizations across 20 sectors.
2. **Compute velocity change** for each org. Flag anything above +50%.
3. **Cross-check with contributor delta.** If velocity is up but contributors are flat, it's probably one person overworking. Filter out.
4. **Check infrastructure repo creation.** If present, flag as higher confidence.
5. **Surface the top 10 per sector.** These are the candidates that deserve a warm outreach this week.

The output: a ranked list, refreshed weekly, of startups showing unusual engineering acceleration before they're visible in traditional deal flow.

---

### Why this matters

Most VCs source deals through their network. The Harvard Business Review 2016 survey of 900 VCs found that ~70% of deals come from network connections. The problem: networks are shared. By the time a startup is making the rounds at demo day or landing in your inbox via a warm intro, it's also landing in every other investor's inbox.

Engineering-acceleration signals, by construction, surface companies weeks before they're in anyone's inbox. A startup that's doubled its commit velocity in the last month but hasn't told anyone is exactly the company you want to send a cold-but-informed email to today.

The network still matters — it's how you close. But "find" and "close" are different problems, and the tools for "find" are changing.

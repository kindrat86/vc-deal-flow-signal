# The Velocity Verdict — 12-Minute Perfect Webinar Script

**Product:** GitDealFlow by The Data Nerd
**Framework:** Russell Brunson's Perfect Webinar (Secret #22 — DotCom Secrets)
**Total Runtime:** ~12 minutes
**Format:** YouTube video — screen recordings, chart overlays, synthetic voiceover
**Tone:** Analytical, quiet confidence, no hype. The product is the protagonist.

---

## VISUAL CONVENTIONS FOR THIS SCRIPT

| Cue | Meaning |
|-----|---------|
| `[B-ROLL: ...]` | Stock footage or abstract visual to play under voiceover |
| `[SCREEN: ...]` | Screen recording of the actual product/dashboard |
| `[CHART: ...]` | Data visualization overlay (line chart, bar, Gini distribution) |
| `[TEXT: ...]` | On-screen text overlay (key quote, stat, or takeaway) |
| `[CUT TO:]` | Hard cut to new visual |
| `[ZOOM:]` | Zoom in/out of current visual |
| `[LOWER THIRD:]` | Lower-third text banner |

**Color palette:** Dark background (#0a0a0a), accent (#00d4aa/teal), text white. No face, no talking head — screen-forward throughout.

## PART 3: THREE SECRETS (6:00)

### 3:00 — Secret #1: The Vehicle — "GitHub data is just noise." (2:00)

`[SCREEN: A chaotic GitHub commit graph — hundreds of tiny squares, randomized, visually noisy. The word "NOISE?" appears over it in large red text. Then the graph reorganizes, pulling out a single company's trajectory — a clear spike from a flat baseline.]`

`[TEXT: "Secret #1: The Vehicle"]`

**VO:** Objection number one. The vehicle objection. "GitHub data is just noise."

Fair objection. Raw commit counts are noisy. A bot can inflate them. A hackathon can spike them for a weekend. A single developer pushing config files looks the same on the surface as a team shipping features.

`[SCREEN: Side-by-side comparison. Left — a single dot jumping randomly (labeled "Raw commit count"). Right — a rolling 14-day window with a baseline band, showing a sharp breakout above the band (labeled "Velocity deviation from baseline").]`

We do not look at absolute numbers.

`[TEXT: "We measure acceleration from baseline. Not volume."]`

We look at acceleration patterns — when a company's engineering velocity deviates sharply from its own baseline.

`[CHART: Line chart. X-axis: 12 weeks. Y-axis: commits per 14 days. A flat line for weeks 1–10, then a sharp 3.2x spike in weeks 11–12. The spike is highlighted in teal. Label: "Regime change — not noise."]`

That is not noise. That is a regime change. Something happened inside that company. They hired. They found product-market fit. They are preparing to launch.

`[SCREEN: Three real startup cards from the dashboard — company name, sector, velocity surge percentage. Each card flips to show the outcome: "Announced Series A — 34 days later."]`

Let me show you what this looks like in the wild.

`[SCREEN: GitDealFlow dashboard — filter to "Deploy Frequency Spike." Three cards appear: +573% (Enterprise SaaS), +421% (Data Infrastructure), +520% (Gaming). Camera slowly pans across them.]`

**VO:** These are this week's top movers — real companies, real acceleration, measured against their own baselines. A 573 percent velocity surge on a 14-day window. That is not a bot. That is a team that just scaled.

`[CHART: Bar chart showing the false positive rate. Label: "Velocity alone: 41% false-positive rate on Series A prediction within 60 days." A second bar adds a second condition — low Gini coefficient. Label: "Velocity + contributor diversity: 3.4× lift. False-positive rate drops by half."]`

But here is the part most people miss: velocity alone is not enough.

`[TEXT: "Velocity matters. The SHAPE of the velocity matters more."]`

Velocity matters. But the shape of the velocity matters more. And that brings me to the second objection.

### 5:00 — Secret #2: The Internal — "I already have enough deal flow." (2:00)

`[SCREEN: An inbox. Email after email: "Warm intro: [Startup Name]", "Fwd: Deck for your review", "Following up on the round." The inbox fills, scrolls, fills again. All the emails look the same. Then the screen dims and one line appears.]`

`[TEXT: "Secret #2: The Internal Belief"]`

**VO:** Objection number two. The internal belief. "I already have enough deal flow from my network."

Here is the uncomfortable truth. Your network shows you what other investors are already seeing.

`[SCREEN: A split-screen visualization. Left: "Your network" — 5 investor silhouettes all looking at the same deck. Right: "The 21–47 day window" — one investor silhouette approaching a founder at a laptop, ahead of the pack.]`

By the time a warm intro reaches you, the founder has talked to three to five other investors. The deck is circulating. The terms are forming. You are competing on reputation and speed — not on information.

`[TEXT: "You're not early. You're just in line."]`

The deals that generate outsized returns are the ones where you arrive before consensus forms. Before the deck exists. Before the company is "hot." That is the 21-to-47-day window.

`[SCREEN: Timeline. Week 0: "Signal fires — commit velocity surges, contributors spike." Week 1–3: "You reach out. You're the only investor in the conversation." Week 4–6: "Deck circulates. 5+ investors now involved." Week 7: "Round announced. Press covers it. Allocation is gone."]`

`[The Week 1–3 segment pulses in teal. Label: "Your window."]`

**VO:** Engineering accelerates before the fundraise starts. In that window you can reach out first, offer help before they need money, and build a relationship before everyone else is trying to.

`[CHART: Gini coefficient distribution. X-axis: 0.0 to 1.0. Two distributions overlaid. One labeled "Series A within 60 days" — clustered around 0.15–0.30. One labeled "No round" — spread wide, heavy tail toward 0.60+. Highlighted region: Gini < 0.30.]`

`[TEXT: "Gini coefficient < 0.30: the work is distributed. The team is real."]`

Now here is the signal that separates the real teams from the noise. When a velocity surge is paired with low top-contributor concentration — a Gini coefficient under 0.30 — you are looking at a team, not a founder working alone at 2 a.m.

`[SCREEN: Two side-by-side contributor pie charts. Left: One large slice (80%) — "1 developer doing 80% of commits." Labeled "⚠️ Gini 0.72 — likely solo founder." Right: Eight roughly equal slices — "8 developers sharing the load." Labeled "✓ Gini 0.22 — team scaling."]`

A team where one developer does 80 percent of the commits can spike just as hard as a team where eight developers share the load. But only one of those teams looks like a fundraise candidate to a Series A partner.

`[SCREEN: The 3.4x finding — statistical output from the SSRN regression. Highlighted row: "Velocity × Contributor Diversity → 3.4× Series A likelihood (p < 0.01)."]`

When both conditions fire — velocity surge and low Gini — the company is 3.4 times more likely to announce a Series A within 60 days than velocity alone would predict. That single composite is the most predictive signal in the entire 219-observation panel.

`[TEXT: "3.4× more likely. One composite signal. Public data."]`

But none of this matters if you believe public data cannot give you an edge.

### 7:00 — Secret #3: The External — "Public data can't be an edge." (2:00)

`[SCREEN: A trading floor — Bloomberg terminals, scrolling tickers, traders at desks. Fade to: the Renaissance Technologies logo. Then: one line of text.]`

`[TEXT: "Secret #3: The External Belief"]`

**VO:** Objection number three. The external belief. "Public data cannot be an edge — everyone has access to it."

`[TEXT: "Renaissance Technologies. Founded 1988. Data: Reuters quotes. SEC filings. OPRA ticks. All public."]`

Renaissance Technologies started in 1988 on data anyone could buy. Reuters market quotes. OPRA options ticks. SEC filings. Every fund on Wall Street had access to the same feed.

`[TEXT: "Medallion Fund: ~39% net annualized. 30 years. Best multi-decade return in recorded history."]`

Thirty years later, the Medallion Fund had compounded at roughly 39 percent net of fees — the best multi-decade fund return in recorded history.

`[TEXT: "The data wasn't the edge. The LENS was the edge."]`

The data was not the edge. The lens was the edge.

`[SCREEN: Back to the fintech story. The GitHub org page — three founders, one repo, commit graph spiking. Side panel: "October 2024. 3 other angels had GitHub accounts. 0 opened this page that month."]`

Now apply that to a Saturday in October 2024. I knew three other angels who wrote cheques at the same size I did. They all had GitHub accounts. None of them opened that organization's page that month. I checked.

`[SCREEN: Side-by-side tool comparison table. Columns: GitDealFlow, Harmonic, Tracxn, Affinity, PitchBook. Rows: "Publishes methodology" (only GitDealFlow checked), "Exposes raw data" (only GitDealFlow checked), "Engineering signal" (only GitDealFlow), "Price" (€49/mo vs €1k–€10k/mo).]`

Three weeks later the team announced a four-million-dollar Series A. The two investors who got in had either been told by a warm intro — fine, but slow — or had been reading the same public data I had. The other three later said they "missed" the round. They did not miss it. They did not read it.

Right now, zero investor tools package GitHub activity as a dedicated deal-flow signal. Harmonic, Tracxn, Affinity — none of them publish their methodology. None expose raw data. Engineering signal is the open lane.

`[TEXT: "Same as SEC filings. Same as Reuters. Same as every market that ever produced alpha out of public data."]`

The data is public. The lens is the edge. And the lens costs 49 euros a month.

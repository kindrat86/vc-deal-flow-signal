**Context:** Author argues most indie hackers waste time seeking the "perfect idea" when the real challenge is committing to a boring, obvious problem and executing. Zero comments.

**Comment to post:**

> This is backed up by what I see in the data. I look at engineering activity across hundreds of startups, and the ones with the strongest traction signals are almost never the "clever idea" ones. They're solving painfully obvious problems — monitoring, billing, scheduling, data cleanup — with relentless consistency.
>
> The pattern I keep seeing: the boring-problem startups have steady, accelerating commit velocity over months. The "brilliant idea" startups have a big initial burst and then activity drops off. Execution regularity correlates with traction far more than idea novelty.
>
> I think the trap is that "finding the right idea" feels like productive work. It's comfortable. You can spend months in research mode and feel busy without ever shipping anything that gets rejected. The moment you commit to a boring problem and start shipping, you get real feedback, and real feedback is uncomfortable.
>

## Comment 5: "I help founders get their first 100 users"

**Link:** https://www.indiehackers.com/post/i-help-founders-get-their-first-100-users-heres-what-i-ve-learned-252734737f
**Context:** Distribution strategies for B2B founders. Validate before building, start distribution early, concentrate on one channel. 4 likes, 42 comments — active discussion.

**Comment to post:**

> The "one channel, deeply worked" advice is the most underrated thing here. The data backs it up.
>
> I track engineering activity and growth patterns across a lot of early-stage products, and the ones that hit first 100 users fastest have a really specific pattern: they launch in one community, get deeply embedded there, and don't expand to channel two until channel one is producing consistent results. The founders who try to be everywhere — Reddit, Twitter, IH, Product Hunt, newsletters — simultaneously end up with thin presence everywhere and traction nowhere.
>
> The other thing I've noticed: the startups that get to 100 users fastest tend to have landing pages live before their first meaningful commit. The distribution thinking starts before the building, not after. It changes what you build, because you're already getting signal from the landing page about what resonates.
>
> This is solid advice. The tactical question for most founders: how do you pick which one channel to go deep on? Is it wherever you already have credibility, or wherever your ICP spends the most time?
